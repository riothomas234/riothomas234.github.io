A babylonian maths problem

Mathematics is a subject that most of us have studied at some point. It is loved and hated by many. As a subject, it has long held prestige with scholars due to its ideal and eternal aspects but also as a treasure trove of application. Beginning in the melting pot of Mesopotamia, mathematics has a rich history coursing through the golden age of ancient greece to the refinement of the Renaissance. This collection of ideas that often feels contrived to some has been around for a long time. Why do we care about mathematics and study it today?

Consider the subject's history. We have evidence of counting from the tally marks of hunter gathers etched in bone tracing back to the paleolithic era, presumably used for counting possessions and food. With the agricultural revolutio happening around 10000 B.C.E, trading and the ownership of land grew traction. Our oldest record of writing is the code of Hammurabi which are a set of laws etched into clay tablets for the citizens of Babylon, in modern day Iran. Writing facilitated the wider spread of information, acting as a foundation for larger civilisations to grow, under the rule of heavy handed kings. Maintaining kingdoms required taxes and prosperous trade, which meant goods needed to be accounted for and land given out accurately. Arithmetic was the tool for this, which we learn in primary school today; every method taught to us would have had equivalents in ancient Babylon. Concepts of geometry like the areas and volumes of different shapes were found empirically in order to measure plots of land and build pyramidic structures like the ziggurat.

Astrology had a foothold in Babylon acting as channel for gods to communicate their intentions with the citizens. The times and lengths of eclipses gave themselves to mysterious meanings and were tracked in complex star charts. While the mathematics of today, a subject of logical rigour and debate, only emerged in ancient greece, the foundations of mathematics as a practical discipline were layed in Ancient Mesopotamia.

Quadratic equations were present here but in a very different form to the way we write them now. Consider the Yale Babylonian Collection’s tablet, YBC 6967 written in cuneiform shown below:

The exact translation is as follows:

The igi.bi over the igi 7 is beyond.
The igi and the igi.bi are what?
You:
7 the the igi.bi over the igi is beyond
to two break, then 3 30.
3 30 with 3 30 let them eat each other, then 12 15
To 12 15 that came up for you
1, the field, add, then 1 12 15.
The equalside of 1 12 15 is what? 8 30.
8 30 and 8 30, its equal, lay down, then
3 30, the holder,
from one tear out,
to one add.
one is 12, the second 5.
12 is the igi.bi, 5 the igi.

The igi and igi.bi are the sides of a rectangle with area 60. ‘The igi.bi over the igi 7 is beyond.’ tells us that one side is 7 units longer than the other. What are the sides?

The Babylonian solution to this problem is derived geometrically. Let igi.bi be x and igi be y. The rectangle below has sides x and y and comprises of a yellow square and a purple rectanlge. Let the area of the big rectanglebe 60 units squared.

We can half the smaller yellow rectangle into even smaller ones of side lengths y and 3.5. The babylonians used a base-60 system, so the way they measured lengths is similar to how we measure time today, i.e. 3 and half hours is 3 hours 30 mins; 3 and half metres is 3 30 metres. So

‘to two break, then 3 30’

gives us the side of the new rectangles, 3.5.

We can bring one of the halves to the top of the square as shown below.

The babylonians called this shape a ‘gnomon’. We turn it into a bigger square by inserting a smaller square of side 3.5 into the corner.

‘3 30 with 3 30 let them eat each other, then 12 15’.

A square with side 3.5 indeed has an area of 12.25 or 12 15 in base-60.

‘To 12 15 that came up for you
1, the field, add, then 1 12 15.’

The area of the initial large rectangle was 60 units squared. But in base-60 this is 1. The area of the new square below is the sum of the area of the initial rectangle and the area of the green square we added. 60+12.25=72.25 which is 1 12 15 here.

‘The equalside of 1 12 15 is what? 8 30.’

The Babylonians would have been able to compute the square root of a number in the context of finding the sides of a square with a given area. Here the side length of our new square is the square root of 72.25, 8.5.

‘8 30 and 8 30, its equal, lay down, then
3 30, the holder,
from one tear out,
to one add.
one is 12, the second 5.
12 is the igi.bi, 5 the igi.’

From the square above, we can see that y or igi is the side of the new square minus 3.5 to give 5. x is y+7 or the side of the new square plus 3.5 to give 12. Hence ‘12 is the igi.bi, 5 the igi.’ Today, we would have solved this problem by algebraic means with a technique called ‘completing the square’.

\begin{align*} y(y+7) &= 60 \\[2pt] y^{2} + 7y &= 60 \\[6pt] y^{2} + 7y + \left(\tfrac{7}{2}\right)^{2} &= 60 + \left(\tfrac{7}{2}\right)^{2} \\[6pt] \left(y + \tfrac{7}{2}\right)^{2} &= 60 + 12.25 = 72.25 \\[6pt] y + \tfrac{7}{2} &= \pm\, 8.5 \\[2pt] y &= -3.5 \pm 8.5 \end{align*}

Hence, quadratics existed in 1600 B.C. before algebra found its roots during the islamic golden age of 11th century A.D. Even up to the renaissance, quadratic and cubic equations were solved by trying to make squares and cubes or by physically measuring the distance between the intersections of shapes. In the context of geometry, there exists no square with a ‘negative’ area whose side we can find. Hence, any time a negative area or volume cropped up, we dismissed it as nonsensical. The more general framework of algebra was required for us to discover and harness the power of imaginary numbers.

Travel blog - Kerala

This earth has pockets of immense beauty. Today, we appreciate this - we have UNESCO and conservation sites. some of these are places where generations of human lives have passed. Do people so embedded in these locations know and appreciate the beauty around them?! I got some answer to this question while travelling to kerala. It was more the reminiscence the trip brought, of my time living here for 5 years as a kid. To an outsider, Kerala is a state of exceedingly many varieties of flora and fauna, from towering coconut trees you shouldn't stand under to cave dwelling tigers and monkeys. But humans tend to view the world asking what use each item is to them. A psychological lens is formed, where you give names to the cassava and jaggernaut crops to eat, the rubber and nutmeg plants that gift you commodities to sell. But the vast forest you pass on the walk to your shop everyday is a speck in your consciousness, never having had to venture out in it, nor it encroaching into your daily life. To those who've never lived anywhere else, all of this are the usual features of life. Consider like the islamic population who lived next to the ancient pyramids in Egypt for centuries but never knew who built them or why.

Staying in Kerala feels like living in a certain slice of consciousness, with specific types of personalities and specific types of actions you can carry out. To someone who's lived in england for 15 years, it feels quite isolating to not be exposed to the types of people that malayalees have not had the chance to become. Similarly, I think people in the west miss out on understanding what it's like to live in the undeveloped world - where your daily bread is not guaranteed and lack of communication limits your experience to a 10 mile radius. Malayalees are conservative. They're very slow to change. When me and my cousins went to kottayam, the whole town closed by 10pm. Religion has a strong grasp here, where while it is good that people don't stray off this path into much worse ones, they also miss out on the chance for better experiences.

The layout of roads and towns in Kerala is very different to anywhere i've seen before. The path from Kochi to Kottayam weaves through a number of shop clusters, with no highways. This is because kerala's geography and abundant water supplies enabled families to live in highly dispersed plots across the landscape. Normally water is tight and families have to cluster around a communal well. in Kerala, every homestead could build up its own private well, removing any pressure to group around a communal source. The region's fertile land made decentralised, homebased agriculture viable. Each family's parambu was a horticultural plot, cultivating coconut palms, jackfruit and mango trees. There was also not much conflict that pushed families to cluster together into walled forts.

Ibn Battuta was a traveller who left home in Morocco in 1325 to go on a pilgrimage to Mecca. He ended up travelling for 30 years through the Middle East, India, Southeast Asia and China. In this time, Calicut was a significant port connecting China to the middle east. It was a major trading hub of black pepper and ginger.

'On this road, which, as we have said, extends for a two months' march, there is not a foot of ground but is cultivated. Every man has his own orchard, with his house in the middle and a wooden palisade all round it.'

When roads were built, they sliced through this tapestry of private compounds. Lacking a central market, families turned front facing boundaries of private gardens into shops facing the passing traffic. This is a ribbon development where towns bleed into villages, called a desakota model by scholars.

Social bonds are stronger in Kerala than in the west. The pros are that you are less lonely and have support networks when things go wrong, e.g. financial struggles. The cons are that you might have little choice about who you end up living with. This is especially the case for some arranged marriages where the bride has little say in who she marries. I also think there is less individual expression with social ties these strong. Strong groups promote conservatism because outlier behaviours can cause social shame. You also fall into groupthink more often.

Regarding the nature of work: imagine all your possessions are taken away and you are put in an environment with bare necessities. You use them to survive and this shapes work. Sowing seeds, milking the cow. It's a slow life and you might imagine this to be a good punishment that takes away your cool productivity apps and internet access.

The work im doing now is very separated from my immediate surroundings and needs. I inherit the technology humans have created up in history to do my work. I feel like i'm standing on the shoulders of giants. This also validates what i'm doing because continuing someone else's github repo necessarily limits how wrong the work im doing is compared to my predecessors. In india, doing work that is so connected to your immediate surroundings feels a bit surreal. Like I could be totally mistaken in what i'm spending my time doing and there is not external mechanisms to correct me.

You would think with how long we've done manual work like agriculture for, that it'd be very hard for humans to switch the type of work they do. But we see people changing work easily when new technology becomes available. Its a paradox that we've been stuck doing the same type of work for so long but now it feels obvious that we can do it better and so we do. Our nature of work hasn't been limited by our abilities but by the resources available to us. And the resources available to us have been the same for so long.

I'm used to going to an office and working, but for many people workplaces look very different. A lot of people own stores and just sit in them all day. Others have land with crop or pasture and intuitively know what needs to be done on each day for steady maintenance. Long ago, merchants like my great grandfather would travel for months on bullock carts, transporting spices from one place to another - today, the equivalent is a lorry driver. A lot of people just inherit their jobs from their parents. Some of these jobs give little space for creativity or for one to express themselves.

This raises an interesting question about we view our relationship to work in the west. Is our work important in defining who we are? How can this be the case when millions of past generations have been stuck doing the same jobs like farming? I used to think that work does define you - there is a perfect job for me out there and i need to find it. But now, I place less emphasis on the actual work I'm doing and care more about the process of work. The focusing of energy towards pursuing a task causes one to grow, or as the Bhagavad Gita frames it, to find God.

Social media?

I started writing the following past my bedtime. Re reading it, it sounds a lot like a chain of thought trace from Claude.

I'd gotten off social media a few years ago because it was doing things to my brain which i wasn't a fan of. But now after maturing and becoming an adult, I'm more confident i can control what i do on these apps and want to get back on one. Plus, I have more friends now and want to see what they get up to.

I'm interested in different types of social media and which ones to get back on. Ideally there is a social media where i only see what my friends are up to. What their current goals in life are- and maybe i can talk to them further about it. I dont really want to see posts from people i dont follow. What are my options?

Do i want to get news from social media? The risk is that i get a lot of shallow posts that hit my dopamine centers but dont really deliver any real substance. e.g. imagine i get a video of a rally against ai acceleration in westminster. Ok thats cool information. But then i might also just recieve a video of a news host blazing against trump - even if i agree with the sentiment, the post is delivering no real information. however id be drawn to watch the post because it hits my dopamine centers. This is a waste of time. Overall, with knowledge of such algorithms, I'm not going to use social media as a news source and stick to the economist instead.

If I want social media to help my friendships survive i should clarify what a friendship is meant to be. A friendship between two people is meant to facilitate the growth of both persons, so that they can support each other in hard times and appreciate each other in good times. Erich Fromm lays out four pillars in The Art of Loving which i try to follow: care, knowledge, responsibility and respect.

  • Knowledge is deducing that the other might be feeling a certain way, depressed or joyful, based on social cues. In my opinion, any intelligence you might cultivate studying maths at school is aimed at improving one's ability to perform this supreme task.
  • Care is an active concern for the other person's growth. Like realising they want to be a better public speaker and so giving them feedback or pointing them to useful resources.
  • Responsibility is acting when the other is in emotional or physical distress. It's knowing your friend is feeling depressed and making the choice that you want to do something about it rather than ignore it, which is much easier.
  • Respect is the desire to see the other person become who they are meant to be, without projecting any of my own certainties onto the process.

Knowledge is the bedrock of the latter three aspects and what social media should be primed to enable. I want to know what my friends are gunning for at the moment, what they've achieved. I want to learn their ups and their downs. Where they're travelling and what hobbies they're picking up. I want to see them when they feel beautiful and when they're around those they love. Then i can choose to reach out and learn more or provide any help I'm capable of.

The biggest barrier to social media being useful is to actually get my friends on these platforms and to post on them. So, I'll consider the big sites like Instagram, facebook etc where people will already be on. I'm very happy with instagram's following only feed where they only show me posts from the people i follow. I dont think they had this 2-3 years ago, and i deleted the app because i started getting reels on my feed without my asking for them, and i'd decided to get off reels to save my brain. But now i think instagram would be a good candidate. To be honest i never used facebook, it always felt before my time, sorry older friends:( There are also things like substack, linkedin and twitter. Substack is cool - I like reading essays from people i haven't met before on topics I haven't encountered. But a lot of friends aren't on the app or into writing:( Substack could be an adjacent tool to use alongside instagram. And a great way to connect with people i know who are on it. Twitter and Linkedin also have different media types to what I'm really looking for here, but still useful, so they might be adjacently used.

Regarding any criterion for the type of content i want to see from people i know, the analysis above implies that any posts from friends are welcome. The next question is: who do i want to see content from? We have celebrities, acquaintances, friends i've lost touch with, current best friends and so on. I'm happy for anyone to follow me on instagram if they're interested for whatever reason, but who do i spend my precious time focusing on?

Personally, I'm not interested in seeing celebrities' posts on my feed so I'll make sure not to follow any. The only reason to follow an influencer would be because there is something they do that I want to emulate, e.g. maybe they're a really nice person and i want to be like them. For me this type of information comes across better on youtube rather than on short instagram posts. So ill push influencers off my instagram feed. Note that if you're into fashion etc. then short influencer posts might be perfect to inspire you and so might be good to see.

I know I want to follow friends I'm in contact with now. But what about those I've lost contact with? I've moved schools a lot, so there are a lot of old friends i've havent talked to in years. I think you'd want to follow people you've lost contact with because having known them once is a good reason to establish contact with them again. Why would you not establish contact with someone? I might not do so because I'm scared or lazy but it allows for the possibility in the future. What about people i don't like or am ambivalent about? Regarding the latter, it's always better to be friends with someone than to be ambivalent, so you could always reach out and convert an ambivalent person into a friend or someone you don't like. The next part is tricky. Do you follow people you don't like? What does it mean to 'dislike' someone anyway?

It's more complicated than to say disliking is the opposite of liking someone. Philosophers, I'm not sure who, have said that hate is not the opposite of love but instead the same thing. You hate someone when your love is not returned. The love i mean here is a desire for attention and to be loved. When you don't get that love, the only way to maintain self importance is to believe the object you wanted love from was bad anyway and thats why they don't love you. Hence like becomes dislike. However i dont think real love is the pleasure you feel when you recieve attention, but rather the feeling when you give to someone else, unconditional of whether they return it. In the ideal case, hate ceases to be. If hate isn't the opposite of love then perhaps it's ambivalence, others say. But we addressed ambivalence above. You have to make the choice to become friends with someone rather than remain ambivalent.

There can still be people whose actions are destructive and don't appear to be tending towards positive outcomes according to your own beliefs. E.g. I might see someone shouting racist slurs on the street and want them to stop. But I'm not sure if what i feel in this instance is 'hate' per se. It's more a sort of understanding that what I'm seeing in front of me needs to stop and that I should do something about it, e.g. tell the person to stop shouting racist slurs. But i don't feel hate, i just feel like some misunderstanding has happened and needs to be resolved and i feel upset about this.

This means i still ought to follow people i know personally that I 'dislike', because actually i supposedly still like them. I should even follow people i know whose actions i disagree with, in the hope that i can get them to change their actions. I'll probably draw the line at following people i dont personally know whose content i don't think is right, because there's a low chance i can get them to change and now my feed is just making me upset.

To conclude, I've said I want to follow people I personally know but not any celebrities. I want to follow people who i've had even the slightest contact with in the hope of getting to know them more, even if i dislike them or disagree with them. Let's see how that goes:)

Anatomy of a joke

One of the main types of social bonding manifests itself in the joke. Since the paleolithic era (probably?) humans have used humour to build connections with others. Jokes are, of course, funny and laughing is a pleasure. But what goes into a joke and what might be some of the other reasons that they dominate human interactions today as they have done for centuries before?

There is the theory that every joke is a form of benign violation. Most jokes are in essence a violation of something: another person, expectation, yourself… these are the common ones. You see your friend fall down the stairs but they are unhurt. You laugh because a violation has happened to them, a physical transgression (and perhaps a careless slip of mind?), however, because they are unhurt, the violation is benign and so the situation is deemed as being funny. On the other hand, if your friend has seriously hurt himself, it is funny no longer and you must be quite a cruel person to find this laughable. Then there is the roast, where you violate someone else but in a benign way. The line between what is benign and what is malignant is a fine one and varies differently with people. What may be bordering outrageous at the networking dinner from work is vanilla to the underground comedy club. The master satirist can find this line and push it to the limit. The pun employs the violation of expectation. You use a word in a context where, unexpectedly, it’s other meaning also works in perhaps a more twisted way… This is a surprise and the violation of expectation makes us laugh.

Why do we find these forms of benign violation funny? One of the mysteries that befuddled the great philosopher of antiquity, Aristotle, was that of tickling. How was it you could not help bawling your eyes out when someone else tickled you, yet tickling yourself yielded no such effect? Is it a physiological effect where your body reacts differently to foreign skin touching it rather than its own? Experiments have been conducted where the subject was given remote control of a robot hand that tickled them. When they had complete control of the robot hand, its tickling had no effect on them. But when researchers programmed the hands to perform short unexpected spurts of activity not controlled by the subject, they began to start laughing. It seems as if the contact on your body that is not in your control is subconsciously interpreted by your brain as a violation. Yet, your conscious self knows that your older sibling tickling you is not actually a threat. Your brain ends up perplexed by this violation that is not really a violation and it releases this tension by laughing. Hence, tickling, which could easily be thought of as a physiological phenomenon is actually a mental one and it implies that the benign violation formulation of a joke has psychological foundations.

It is then a question of whether the joke is sufficiently enough of a violation that renders it as funny or not. Jokes are important in social contexts where you have recently met a person who seems a suitable candidate as a friend. The next level of friendship is attained where one is able to comfortably roast the other and for them to find it funny. In fact, this may be a type of test, where you are checking how emotionally strong they are: if they can give as good as they can take, it is unlikely they will become emotional baggage for you in the future. Instead, if they are hurt by a light tease, you are turned off almost immediately. This has evolutionary roots: as a caveman, food was scarce and you needed to be quick on your feet. You did not want to accept the foreigner into your tribe if you knew they would become a drain on your precious resources and add nothing back to the tribe. In addition to this, violations are dangerous, but in their benign form can be exciting. This explains the dark humour often exchanged among groups of friends, quickly becoming quite racist and sexist especially among boys. It is not necessarily that people hate minority groups but instead like the thrill of talking about things they’re not allowed to - because it is dangerous and, by extension, exciting. Ironically, normalising racism and sexism could be the best way to put an end to such banter among boys - there is nothing exciting about racist slang if you’re allowed to use it.
